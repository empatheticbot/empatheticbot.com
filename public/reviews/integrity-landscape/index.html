An independent website review · August 2026

A portfolio you can actually look at.

This is the most carefully maintained site in the group I looked at this month. A hundred and twelve pages, every one of them live, ninety-two distinct titles, a separate page for each paver and each wall system, and service-area pages that already exist. The constraint is not neglect. It is that the whole thing was built for a 1,160-pixel monitor, and the customer standing in their own back yard trying to picture a patio is holding a phone.

The short version

The site is fast, stable, and better organised than almost anything its age. Its largest constraint is that the phone gets a different, much smaller website—one with no photographs of your work on it—and that neither version lets a visitor zoom in.

Highest-value opportunities

Give the phone the same company the monitor gets.

Picture somebody in Northville standing in their own back yard on a Saturday, phone in hand, trying to decide whether the space in front of them wants a patio or a retaining wall—and which of two contractors to call. These are ordered by what they cost that person, not by how easy they are to fix.

  1. 01

    High impact · Mobile experience

    Let people zoom in.

    Both versions of the site switch pinch-to-zoom off. The desktop page declares width=1160, maximum-scale=1.0—a fixed layout wider than any phone, with the zoom ceiling pinned—and the mobile page declares maximum-scale=1.0, user-scalable=no outright. On a business selling things people choose by looking closely at texture and colour, the one gesture a customer will reach for is the one that has been disabled. It is also an accessibility failure in its own right: anyone who enlarges text to read it cannot, and it is the specific thing that drags the accessibility score to 74.

    Opportunity: remove the zoom ceiling on both, and let the layout respond to the screen rather than being drawn at a fixed width and scaled.

  2. 02

    High impact · Mobile content parity

    Give the phone the same website.

    Phones are served a separate site. The desktop homepage is 15,744 bytes and renders about 3,969 pixels tall, with six photographs of finished work on it. The mobile homepage is 7,854 bytes and 1,378 pixels tall, and contains no photographs of finished work at all—a logo, a header banner, and three interface icons. A landscaping company’s entire argument is how the work looks, and the version most visitors see does not make it. Separately: your robots.txt contains Disallow: /index.php?view=mobile, so the mobile site is also hidden from search engines.

    Opportunity: one site that reflows, rather than two that diverge. Every visitor gets the photographs, the catalog and the service pages, and there is only one copy of anything to keep current.

  3. 03

    High impact · The portfolio

    Show work from this decade.

    The galleries hold 111 photographs across landscape, hardscape, and decks and pergolas. The remodels section returned none on its first page. The newest filenames that carry a date are from June 2015; several images still have a camera date stamp burned into the corner reading 07/07/2009. Meanwhile the newest and most expensive lines you sell—porcelain pavers, Belgard outdoor fireplaces and kitchens—have product pages but almost no installed work behind them. On the homepage, the six service photographs are 350 pixels wide, which is smaller than a phone screen; the largest gallery images are 800 pixels.

    Opportunity: photographs at a size worth judging, and a habit of adding a handful from each job—so the gallery shows what the crew built last season rather than what it built eleven years ago.

  4. 04

    High impact · Search visibility

    Describe the expensive things.

    Reading all 112 pages in the sitemap: 92 have their own title, which is genuinely good work. But 17 have no meta description at all, and they are not a random 17. They are porcelain-pavers and its eight product pages, and belgard-outdoor-fireplaces-kitchens and its six. Every page for the highest-ticket work you do is the page a search engine has nothing to quote from. A further 19 pages—the paged photo galleries—share one identical title, “Landscaping Photos, Plymouth, Northville, Canton, South Lyon, Livonia, MI.”

    Opportunity: a sentence of description on each of those 17 pages, written for someone searching “porcelain paver patio Northville,” and gallery pages named for what is actually in them.

  5. 05

    Medium impact · Small broken things

    The fax number is a phone link.

    In the mobile footer, both numbers are tap-to-call links—including the fax: tel:734-259-5056. Somebody on a phone taps the line labelled Fax and calls a fax machine. Alongside it: the header still carries an “Angie’s List Super Service Award” badge, and Angie’s List has been called Angi since 2021, so the badge now advertises a company that no longer exists under that name. The page loads jQuery 1.11.3 and Modernizr 2.8.3, both from 2015, and carries a revisit-after tag that no search engine has read in twenty years.

    Opportunity: the fax as plain text, the badge refreshed or retired, and the 2015 libraries replaced—none of which a visitor will notice, and all of which they would notice the absence of.

  6. 06

    Medium impact · Measurement

    You probably have no traffic data since 2023.

    The analytics on the site is UA-133918948-1—a Universal Analytics property. Universal Analytics stopped processing new data in July 2023. Unless a newer property is running somewhere I cannot see from outside, nothing on this site has been measured for three years: not which service pages people read, not which of the 45 product pages get visited, not how many people reach the estimate form and leave. Every decision about the site since then has been made blind.

    Opportunity: analytics that is actually reporting, so the next change can be judged rather than guessed at—and so you find out which of those 45 product pages is worth photographing first.

Audit details

What the measurements say.

Tests were run against the public site on August 24, 2026, using Lighthouse 12.8.2 with simulated mobile throttling—a phone on roughly a 1.6 Mbps connection with a fourfold processor slowdown. All 112 pages in the sitemap were also fetched and read. Results can vary by network, device, cache state, and future content changes.

74/100

Accessibility

The lowest of the four, and the disabled zoom is the largest reason. Also flagged: images not sized for the screen, touch targets too close together, and links that cannot be distinguished from surrounding text. A complete assessment would still need keyboard, contrast and screen-reader testing by hand.

100/100

SEO

A perfect automated score, and it is deserved on the homepage. What it cannot see is the sitemap-wide picture: 17 pages with no description and 19 sharing a title. Reading all 112 by hand is the only way that turns up.

75/100

Performance

478 KB across 23 requests, the largest image finishing at 4.9 seconds on a simulated phone, blocking time 10 ms. Speed is a preservation goal here, not an argument for a rebuild — and there is room to spend some of it on bigger photographs.

0 shift

Stability

Exactly zero layout shift. Nothing jumps under a reader’s thumb, on a page with a rotating hero slideshow — that is harder than it sounds and worth being careful not to lose.

Early design exploration

One direction, to make the idea concrete.

This is an exploratory concept, not a final design. It uses your own logo, your own photographs, your real address, phone number and service list, and counts taken from your own sitemap. The captions on the gallery images are mine and would be yours to correct. Final copy, photography and any product or manufacturer wording would be reviewed with you before anything was published.

Direction 01

Portfolio first, on any screen

Leads with the work at full width, then the services, then the catalog you already have — and the same page reflows to a phone rather than handing it a smaller site.

A sensible path forward

Unlock the zoom, then bring the photographs forward.

If Integrity Landscape ever takes this on—with anyone—this is roughly the order I would suggest. The first phase is measured in minutes, not days, and one line of it fixes the accessibility score.

  1. 01
    Minutes, not days

    Remove maximum-scale and user-scalable=no from both viewports, unlink the fax number, and put a description on the 17 pages that have none.

  2. 02
    Get measuring again

    Replace the Universal Analytics property so the next three months of decisions are informed, and see which product pages people actually reach.

  3. 03
    One site, photographs first

    Retire the separate mobile version, rebuild as a single responsive site, and re-shoot or re-crop the portfolio so images are worth opening.

  4. 04
    Keep caring for it

    A handful of photographs from each job, seasonal front-page changes from spring design through snow, and somebody watching the badges and the copyright line.
