An independent website review · August 2026

A clearer digital front door for Cascade.

Cascade already has the parts that are hardest to build: decades of focused experience, a real body of patient resources, and a phone number that reaches an actual person. What the current website asks is that visitors find all of that on their own. This review is about closing that gap—mostly on a phone, which is where most people arrive.

The short version

The site is fast and holds a strong body of patient information. Its largest constraint is not speed—it is the work a visitor has to do to understand what Cascade offers and find a useful path.

What is already working

A sound foundation—not a rescue project.

Highest-value opportunities

Help people orient themselves before asking them to explore.

Most people reach a site like this on a phone, at a bad moment—a new diagnosis, a denied claim, a form due tomorrow. These are ordered by what they cost that person, not by how easy they are to fix.

  1. 01

    High impact · Information hierarchy

    Lead with what Cascade helps people do.

    A parent whose child was diagnosed last week arrives and is offered three featured choices: Insurance Help, newsletters, and “What is 340B?” Each one matters to somebody. None of them answers the first question a new patient, caregiver, or referring professional actually has—what does Cascade do, and what do I do next?

    Opportunity: begin with one concise description and a small set of task-based paths: pharmacy, insurance or costs, patient forms, and bleeding-disorder information.

  2. 02

    High impact · Mobile experience

    Design the phone experience as a route, not a stack.

    At 390 pixels wide, the homepage becomes a very long sequence of full-width images and secondary material. Holidays, survey content, testimonials, and coalition information all appear before the footer, while common patient actions require substantial scrolling. Someone standing at a pharmacy counter scrolls past a holiday notice to reach the thing they came for.

    Opportunity: compress the utility header, prioritize three or four tap-friendly tasks, and move time-sensitive or institutional content to clearly labeled secondary sections.

  3. 03

    High impact · Accessibility

    Give every interactive image a meaningful name.

    Lighthouse found three large homepage image links with no discernible accessible name: Insurance Help, newsletters, and “What is 340B?” A screen reader can encounter these as unnamed links even though nearby headings make the intent visually apparent.

    Opportunity: make the heading and image one named link, preserve visible focus treatment, and manually test keyboard and screen-reader flows.

  4. 04

    High impact · Search visibility

    Write a useful search summary for every important page.

    The homepage has no meta description. A review of all 43 sitemap pages found that none supply one. The homepage also lacks Open Graph and social preview metadata, and no structured data was detected there.

    Opportunity: create unique titles and descriptions around real patient questions, add organization and service-area schema, and provide useful previews when pages are shared.

  5. 05

    Medium impact · Content design

    Turn dense resource pages into guided choices.

    Patient Forms includes important enrollment, consent, privacy, and information links, but the mobile page presents them primarily as long bulleted lists. People must interpret similar document names without much guidance about which form applies, what it contains, or what happens next.

    Opportunity: group content by task, explain who each document is for, identify file type and destination, and provide a clear help path when a visitor is unsure.

  6. 06

    Medium impact · Technical quality

    Simplify the front-end and resolve small quality leaks.

    The page loads 41 document, style, script, font, and image requests in the measured run. Google Translate, two analytics paths, Divi, jQuery, and plugin assets add dependencies. The favicon request returned a server error, and the LCP image is discovered through CSS rather than the initial HTML.

    Opportunity: reduce the dependency chain, use responsive modern images, make the hero resource directly discoverable, fix favicon delivery, and preserve the caching that already makes repeat visits fast.

Current experience

The issue is easier to see at a glance.

The desktop page has several competing centers of attention. On mobile, that same content becomes a long linear journey, which magnifies every hierarchy decision.

Audit details

What the measurements say.

Tests were run against the public site on August 13, 2026. Results can vary by network, device, cache state, and future content changes.

96/100

Accessibility

The automated score is encouraging. The named-link failure is concrete and fixable. A complete accessibility assessment would still require keyboard, zoom/reflow, contrast, form, PDF, and assistive-technology testing.

92/100

SEO

Canonicals, titles, headings, and a sitemap are present. The largest audited gap is page presentation: no meta descriptions across the sitemap, plus no structured data or social metadata on the homepage.

213 ms

Observed LCP

The measured cached run was fast and had no meaningful layout shift. Performance is a preservation goal, not the primary redesign argument. There is still room to simplify dependencies and improve first-load resource discovery.

88/100

Best practices

Browser console and cookie issues were reported, including a failed favicon request. These are maintenance signals rather than a crisis, but a rebuild should leave the site quieter and easier to reason about.

Early design exploration

Three ways the same content could feel clearer.

These are exploratory concepts, not final designs. They demonstrate hierarchy, responsive direction, and a calmer path into Cascade’s existing services. Final copy, imagery, and medical or legal language would be reviewed with Cascade.

Direction 01

Editorial and reassuring

A warm introduction followed by three simple service paths.

Direction 02

Community-centered

A human, open composition with quieter resource navigation.

Direction 03

Task-first and direct

A practical front door for visitors who already know what they need.

A sensible path forward

Improve the system, not just the homepage.

If Cascade ever takes this on—with anyone—this is roughly the order I’d suggest.

  1. 01
    Listen and inventory

    Confirm audiences, recurring questions, analytics, content owners, compliance needs, and the full page/document set.

  2. 02
    Reshape the information

    Build a task-based sitemap, define reusable page patterns, and rewrite the critical entry points in plain language.

  3. 03
    Design and build accessibly

    Create responsive templates, migrate content carefully, preserve URLs where appropriate, and validate forms, documents, and third-party handoffs.

  4. 04
    Keep caring for it

    Monitor search, performance, accessibility, analytics, and content freshness after launch instead of treating launch day as the finish line.
