Say where you are, and what you fix.

Four decades of basement work, a lifetime transferable warranty, nine services each explained properly, and one of the best-reviewed reputations of any contractor in the area. The website carries almost none of that in the first ten seconds. It never says Michigan, it says Westland once—in the footer—and the only place the word “waterproofing” appears on the homepage is inside a sentence a customer wrote.

Highest-value opportunities

Put the case for you above the fold.

Picture a homeowner in Livonia who found water along the basement wall after Thursday’s storm. It is nine at night, they have three contractors open in three tabs, and they are deciding who gets the call tomorrow. These are ordered by what they cost that person, not by how easy they are to fix.

  1. 01

    High impact · Search visibility

    Say where you are.

    The homepage title—the blue line in a search result—reads “Basement Waterproofing | Affordable Dry Basement | United States.” For a company working out of a shop on N. Hix, that is the one field where the city belongs and it has been given away to a placeholder. Reading the rendered homepage word by word: “Michigan” never appears. “Westland” appears once, in the footer address. And “waterproofing”—the phrase people actually type—appears exactly once, inside a quotation from a customer. The site name is also inconsistent across pages: five say “Affordable Dry Basement,” four say “affordabledrybasement,” all lowercase and run together, which is what a searcher sees.

    Opportunity: titles and descriptions that name the work and the place—“Basement waterproofing in Westland, Michigan”—and a first paragraph that says the same thing in a sentence a person would say out loud.

  2. 02

    High impact · Trust

    Show the reviews you already have.

    Your reputation is the strongest asset in this review and the site shows three testimonials, typed in by hand, sitting under a heading two-thirds of the way down the page. The heading above them is an <h5>—the smallest kind there is. The link to them in the navigation reads “Testimonals”, and so does that page’s title, so the misspelling is also what appears in a browser tab and a search result. Meanwhile the page loads a Google-reviews widget—266 KB of JavaScript, three quarters of it unused—and no star rating or review count appears anywhere in the rendered text. You are paying the weight for the proof and not displaying it.

    Opportunity: the live rating and count near the top of the page, the most recent reviews pulled automatically so nothing looks hand-picked, and the spelling fixed in the one place everybody sees it.

  3. 03

    High impact · Mobile experience

    Design for the phone people actually hold.

    The mobile site declares a fixed 320-pixel layout, which the browser then scales up by about 1.22× to fill a 390-pixel screen—so everything is drawn slightly larger and slightly softer than it was designed to be, and there is no room to work with. What fills that first screen is a logo with an empty grey field beside it, then two image carousels side by side, each about 130 pixels wide, showing dark photographs of pipework with arrows and dots layered on top of them, then a black box reading CALL NOW. Nothing in the first screen says basement, waterproofing, or Westland.

    Opportunity: a layout that uses the width of a modern phone, one photograph at a size worth looking at instead of two that are not, and the sentence that explains the business where the carousels are now.

  4. 04

    High impact · Performance

    Stop shipping a hundred and nine scripts.

    The homepage makes 199 requests weighing 2,553 KB. Of that, 109 requests and 1,614 KB are JavaScript, and 11 requests and 83 KB are images—so the page carries roughly nineteen times more code than photography, on a site selling work that has to be seen to be understood. 193 of the 199 requests go to somebody else’s servers. The largest image on the screen does not finish drawing until 14.8 seconds on a simulated phone. Text appears at 2.7 seconds, so the page looks like it is working long before it is.

    Opportunity: a build where the photographs are the heavy part. Fewer third-party widgets, images sized for the screen they land on, and the first useful thing on screen in about two seconds rather than fifteen.

  5. 05

    Medium impact · Content accuracy

    Pick a number and keep it.

    Two paragraphs apart, the homepage says you have been solving water problems “since 1985” and, in a badge below it, “30 Years of experience.” Both cannot be right, and the second one stopped being right around 2015—it is quietly costing you eleven years of credibility on a page whose whole argument is longevity. Nearby: “FREE ESTIMATES” appears four times and “Free Estimates” once, and the footer reads “© Proudly created with Wix.com” with no copyright line for the business at all.

    Opportunity: say “since 1985” and let the arithmetic happen automatically, one consistent capitalisation for the offer, and a footer that belongs to the company rather than to the tool that built it.

  6. 06

    Medium impact · Structure and accessibility

    Let the links and headings say something.

    Eight of the nine service cards end in a link reading “More Info”. A screen-reader user pulling up a list of links on the page gets eight identical entries, and a search engine gets no signal about where any of them go. The heading order on the homepage runs h3 → h4 → three h1s—“FREE ESTIMATES”, “(734) - 721 - 0122”, “WHY CHOOSE US?”—none of which names the business or the city. Navigation links point at generated anchors like ./#comp-k9alam04. Two of the eight images carry no description at all, and they are the photographs of your own work. One more, worth checking rather than assuming: the page throws an uncaught TypeError: Cannot read properties of undefined (reading 'conversionId') on every load, and a click-fraud monitoring script is installed—which usually means paid advertising is running. It is worth confirming your conversions are still being recorded.

    Opportunity: links that name their destination, one h1 that says what the business is, descriptions on the photographs, and a look at whether the ad tracking is reporting.

Current experience

The homepage, in full.

The desktop page is orderly. On a phone the same content becomes a narrow column built for a 320-pixel screen, and the decision about what comes first is magnified—two small carousels and a call-to-action, before anything explains the business.

Audit details

What the measurements say.

Tests were run against the public site on August 24, 2026, using Lighthouse 12.8.2 with simulated mobile throttling—a phone on roughly a 1.6 Mbps connection with a fourfold processor slowdown. All sixteen pages in the sitemap were also read by hand. Results can vary by network, device, cache state, and future content changes.

64/100

Performance

The main image finishes at 14.8 seconds and blocking time is 300 ms. Almost all of it is third-party JavaScript rather than anything you wrote. Weight is the cheapest thing on this list to fix, and it is the one that most affects whether a phone visitor waits.

95/100

Accessibility

A high score. What it caught: heading order, touch targets too close together in the menu, and eight links that all read the same. A complete assessment would still need keyboard, zoom, contrast and screen-reader testing by hand.

92/100

SEO

High, and the most misleading number here. Automated checks confirm a title exists; they cannot see that it says “United States,” that the site name changes between pages, or that three of sixteen pages have no description at all.

0.011 shift

Stability

Effectively perfect. Nothing jumps while the page loads, which is worth naming as a preservation goal—it is the sort of quality that quietly disappears in a rebuild unless somebody is watching for it.

Early design exploration

One direction, to make the idea concrete.

This is an exploratory concept, not a final design. It uses your own logo, your own photographs, your own service descriptions, and your real address and phone number. The review cards are placeholders—in a built version they would pull from your Google listing—and the rating shown is the one on your public profile, not something measured here. Final copy, photography and any warranty wording would be reviewed with you.

Direction 01

Local, proven, and specific

Leads with what you do and where, puts the rating and the warranty in the first screen, and shows the work at a size somebody can actually judge.

A sensible path forward

Words first, then weight.

If Affordable Dry Basement ever takes this on—with anyone—this is roughly the order I would suggest. The first phase is text edits inside the site you already have, and it would move more than the redesign does.

  1. 01
    Fix the words

    Put Westland and Michigan in the title, correct “Testimonals,” settle 1985 versus thirty years, and rewrite the eight “More Info” links.

  2. 02
    Surface the proof

    Bring the live Google rating and recent reviews to the top of the homepage, and let them update themselves.

  3. 03
    Rebuild the phone experience

    A full-width layout, photographs of the work at a size worth looking at, far fewer third-party scripts, and a first screen that explains the business.

  4. 04
    Keep caring for it

    Seasonal content when the rain and snowmelt come, new job photographs as they happen, and analytics you can trust before the next change is made.
