Start with the job, not the manufacturer.

Wayne Lawn has the difficult parts already: a real local history, a large equipment catalog, recognizable brands, financing, and a service department that keeps the sale from ending at checkout. The website opens with a rotating manufacturer campaign and asks a customer to choose a department before it helps them choose a machine.

Highest-value opportunities

Help a buyer describe the work first.

Picture a Canton homeowner replacing a mower on a Saturday morning. They know the yard, the gates, the hills, and the budget; they may not know which of eleven brands or 257 zero-turn listings fits. These are ordered by the friction in that decision.

  1. 01

    High impact · First impression

    Put shopping and service ahead of the campaign carousel.

    On desktop and phone, the first large panel is manufacturer artwork. The useful paths—equipment, financing, service, parts, and the category browser—sit above it in a compact menu or below it in the page. A first-time buyer gets a campaign before a sentence about what Wayne Lawn can help them accomplish.

    Opportunity: lead with two plain choices—shop equipment and schedule service—then ask whether the equipment is for a home or a business.

  2. 02

    High impact · Trust and upkeep

    Keep seventy-eight years current.

    The homepage says “Family owned since 1948” and, a few lines later, “Over the past 67 years.” In 2026 that history is seventy-eight years. The footer’s visible phone number is correct, but its link target is literally tel:[site_info_phone]. One stale sentence and one template token make a well-established business feel less maintained than it is.

    Opportunity: use “since 1948” wherever possible, fix the footer link, and put dates, hours, and promotional claims on a small recurring content checklist.

  3. 03

    High impact · Product discovery

    Narrow 257 mowers before showing the wall.

    The zero-turn path opens to 257 results, 24 at a time, across eleven pages. The filters are capable, but they start with industry vocabulary—series, deck size, horsepower, commercial or residential—rather than the job a less experienced buyer can describe. Default sorting is price low to high, which makes price the first comparison even when property size and terrain matter more.

    Opportunity: add a short finder for home versus business, acreage, gates, hills, and preferred fuel before revealing the full filter set.

  4. 04

    High impact · Accessibility and structure

    Give every category and control a name.

    In the rendered homepage, fourteen images have no useful alternative text and forty-five links have no text, aria-label, or image description available to name them. Some are likely hidden carousel controls or duplicated theme elements, but the category browser itself includes image links without names. Heading order also begins with an h2, then an h3, before reaching the page’s only h1.

    Opportunity: name category links by destination, keep one visible task-focused h1, and review hidden carousel controls separately from meaningful content images.

  5. 05

    Medium impact · Sales clarity

    Say what “available” means before the quote.

    Product cards show prices and financing examples, then pair “Get a Quote” with “Call for Availability.” That may accurately describe a catalog rather than live stock, but a buyer cannot tell whether the machine is on the floor, orderable, or simply represented online until they start a lead form or call.

    Opportunity: label availability states in ordinary language and set the expectation that a quote request confirms current stock and final terms.

  6. 06

    Medium impact · Content quality

    Remove the empty shelf.

    The homepage ends a section titled “Blog Articles You Might Find Interesting” with “Posts not found.” Empty modules are small, but this one sits beside repeated featured products and a long company introduction, so the page feels assembled from modules rather than edited around a customer’s next step.

    Opportunity: hide empty feeds automatically and keep only the content that helps somebody shop, service, finance, or visit.

One possible direction

Lead with fit, then let the catalog prove the depth.

This is an exploratory concept, not a final design. These previews show its opening section on desktop and phone. It uses Wayne Lawn’s public logo, product photography, address, phone number, founding date, service story, and equipment categories. It does not claim any item is in stock or invent final pricing.

Direction 01

Task-first equipment buying

Shopping and service become the first two actions; home, business, and service paths narrow the catalog before the products arrive.

A sensible path forward

Fix the front door before rebuilding the store.

The highest-return changes do not require replacing the catalog. They change what a new visitor sees before entering it.

  1. 01
    Correct the trust leaks

    Fix the footer phone target, replace the sixty-seven-year sentence, and hide the empty blog module.

  2. 02
    Name the first choices

    Give category imagery accessible labels and put shop, service, and financing into obvious first-screen actions.

  3. 03
    Add a needs-first finder

    Ask about property, use, terrain, and constraints before presenting hundreds of products and technical filters.

  4. 04
    Clarify availability

    Separate on-floor, orderable, and quote-to-confirm states, then keep seasonal campaigns behind the customer path.
