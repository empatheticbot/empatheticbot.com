Prepared for Vintage Vibes Antiques

An independent website review · August 2026

A website that opens when the shop does.

Vintage Vibes already has the parts that are hardest to build: a real storefront, a logo that tells you exactly what is inside before you read a word, and five days a week of people coming through the door. The website currently says “Coming Soon.” This review is about the distance between those two things—mostly on mobile, which is where people check before they decide to drive over.

What is already working

A good foundation wearing a construction sign.

  • A distinctive logo that already carries antiques, collectibles, and vinyl
  • A perfect automated accessibility score, with an accessibility plugin installed
  • A perfect best-practices score: HTTPS, strict transport security, a quiet console
  • Address, hours, phone, and email present as real tap-to-call and mailto links
  • Explicit image sizes and responsive artwork, so almost nothing shifts while loading
  • A working XML sitemap, canonical URLs, and a skip-to-content link already in place

Highest-value opportunities

Answer the question people are actually asking.

Most people reach a shop like this on a phone, in a car, on a Saturday afternoon, deciding whether it is worth the drive. They want three things: is it open, where is it, and is there anything in there for me. These are ordered by what they cost that person, not by how easy they are to fix.

  1. 01

    High impact · Content design

    Publish one phone number and one address.

    The homepage lists 16320 Middlebelt Rd, Livonia and (734) 469-4045. The Contact page, which is live and listed in the sitemap, lists 248-318-7801 and an address in a different city. Someone who searches for “Vintage Vibes contact” can land on that page first, call a number that is not the shop, and drive somewhere that is not the shop.

    Opportunity: keep the shop’s real details in one place that every page reads from, so a correction only ever has to be made once.

  2. 02

    High impact · Information hierarchy

    Let the site say the shop is open.

    The largest words on the homepage are “Coming Soon!”, followed by a note that the online home is under construction. Everything a visitor needs is already directly underneath—address, hours, phone, email, and a map. The shop is open five days a week, but someone skimming on a phone reads the headline and reasonably concludes it has not opened yet.

    Opportunity: lead with whether the shop is open right now and how to get there, and let what is already on the page do the work it is doing anyway.

  3. 03

    High impact · Content design

    Finish or retire the pages that are still showing template text.

    About and Contact are both publicly reachable and both still display the theme’s placeholder paragraph, beginning “Your content goes here.” WordPress’s default sample post, Hello world!, is also still published and listed in the sitemap. A shopper who finds the About page through search learns nothing about the shop.

    Opportunity: write the two pages that answer real questions—what the shop carries and how to reach it—and unpublish the rest until they say something.

  4. 04

    High impact · Search visibility

    Tell search engines what the shop is and where it is.

    None of the 8 pages in the sitemap has a meta description—the short summary that appears under a result in Google. It was the only check the automated SEO audit failed. There is also no structured data anywhere on the site, which is the machine-readable form of the hours, address, and category that are already printed on the homepage, and no social preview metadata, so sharing the link on Facebook—where the shop already has an audience—produces a blank card.

    Opportunity: write a real description for each page, add local business details in a form search engines can read, and point robots.txt at the sitemap it currently does not mention.

  5. 05

    Medium impact · Mobile experience

    Load the map when someone asks to see it.

    On the simulated mobile run, the largest element on the page took 10.2 seconds to appear, and 6.4 of those seconds were spent waiting rather than downloading. The page pulls 69 requests and roughly 1.3 MB to deliver about forty words, most of it the embedded Google map and two icon fonts totalling about 167 KB. On a phone on a slow connection outside the store, that is a long time to look at nothing.

    Opportunity: show a static map image that opens the real map when tapped, and load only the icons the page actually uses.

  6. 06

    Medium impact · Technical quality

    Clear away the leftovers that make a live site look untended.

    Every page carries a 2025 copyright line. The sitemap also offers search engines an “Uncategorized” category archive and an author archive for a user named admin, neither of which is meant for visitors. Individually these are trivial; together they are the small signals a careful shopper reads as “maybe this place is not around anymore.”

    Opportunity: keep the footer year current automatically, and keep archives that exist for the software out of the map handed to search engines.

Audit details

What the measurements say.

Tests were run against the public site on August 18, 2026, using Lighthouse 12.8.2 with simulated mobile throttling. Results can vary by network, device, cache state, and future content changes.

100/100

Accessibility

A perfect automated score, and an accessibility plugin is actively installed. That is genuinely uncommon and worth protecting. A complete assessment would still need keyboard, zoom, contrast, and screen-reader testing—and a look at the arithmetic challenge on the contact form, which can be a real barrier.

92/100

SEO

Titles, headings, canonical URLs, and a sitemap are all in place. The single failed check was the missing meta description, and that gap repeats across all 8 pages the sitemap lists.

10.2 s

Largest contentful paint

The slowest of the four measurements and, unusually, part of the argument rather than a footnote. Most of it is render delay, not download time, so it is a question of what the page waits for—not of hosting. Layout stability was excellent at 0.022, and that is worth preserving.

100/100

Best practices

HTTPS with strict transport security, no console errors beyond a routine jQuery notice, and no third-party code blocking the main thread. The foundation here is sound; the work is in what the site says, not how it was built.

A sensible path forward

Small steps, in a useful order.

If Vintage Vibes ever takes this on—with anyone—this is roughly the order I’d suggest.

  1. 01
    Correct what is wrong today

    Fix the contact details, retire the placeholder pages and the sample post, and refresh the footer year. This is an afternoon, and it stops the active harm.

  2. 02
    Say what the shop is

    Replace “Coming Soon” with open status, directions, and a plain description of what is on the floor. Write real descriptions for search results.

  3. 03
    Give people a reason to come back

    A simple, regularly updated look at new arrivals—the thing a browsing shop has that a catalogue does not, and the reason someone checks the site twice.

  4. 04
    Keep caring for it

    Watch search, performance, and accessibility after launch, and keep hours and arrivals current, instead of treating launch day as the finish line.
