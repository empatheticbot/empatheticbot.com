Prepared for Canton Center Animal Hospital

An independent website review · August 2026

A website that fits the phone.

Almost five hundred people have taken the time to say this is a good animal hospital, and they name the doctors when they do it. The practice does endoscopy, ultrasound, orthopedic surgery, and it sees rabbits, ferrets, parrots and raptors—which is rare enough that people drive here for it. None of that is reachable from a phone in the state the site is in, and a phone is where somebody with a sick animal starts.

The short version

The site is fast and it is secure—two things that are genuinely hard to fix later. Its largest constraint is that it was built for a desktop monitor and never told a phone what to do with it, so a visitor on a phone sees the whole page drawn at about a third of readable size.

What is already working

A sound practice—and a genuinely fast website.

  • The whole homepage weighs 40 KB across 15 requests—faster than most sites twice its age
  • First paint at 2.5 seconds on a deliberately slowed phone, with nothing blocking it
  • HTTPS everywhere, with http correctly redirecting on both the www and bare domain
  • A real “page not found” page, rather than the server error most hand-built sites return
  • Three after-hours emergency practices published by name, with their phone numbers
  • Exotic-pet depth that almost nobody nearby has: avian, ferrets, rodents, sugar gliders, raptors

Highest-value opportunities

Make the phone version readable, and make it true.

Picture someone in Canton whose rabbit stopped eating this morning. They are standing in the kitchen with a phone in one hand, and they need three things in about twenty seconds: are you open, do you see rabbits, and what is the number. These are ordered by what they cost that person, not by how easy they are to fix.

  1. 01

    High impact · Mobile experience

    Tell the phone how wide the page is.

    None of the twelve pages carries a viewport tag—the one line of markup that tells a phone a page was designed to adapt. Without it, mobile browsers fall back to pretending they are a 980-pixel desktop and then shrink the result to fit. Measured on a 390-pixel screen, the page is drawn at 39% of actual size. The body copy—already 12 pt—lands somewhere near 5 pt. The layout is also 999 pixels wide, so the page scrolls sideways as well as down, and roughly two thirds of the phone screen below the content is empty olive background.

    Opportunity: a layout that reflows to the screen it is on, so the phone number, the hours, and “yes, we see rabbits” are legible without pinching.

  2. 02

    High impact · Content accuracy

    Publish one set of hours.

    The homepage lists “New hours: Mon 8 - 6” through Friday, and Saturday 8 - 2. A second page—ccah_6_002.htm, still live, still indexable—says “Monday - Friday 8:00am - 7pm for regular business.” That page is the COVID-19 update. It still explains curbside handoffs, which door feline and exotic patients use, and that masks are not required, “as of March 1st” of an unnamed year. Nothing on the site links to it any more, which is exactly why nobody has noticed it is wrong—but an orphaned page is not a deleted page, and a search result can land someone on it. Somebody could drive over at 6:30 and find the lights off.

    Opportunity: hours in one place, written once and shown everywhere they appear, and a decision about the COVID page—update it, redirect it, or retire it—rather than leaving it to be found by accident.

  3. 03

    High impact · Search visibility

    Give the homepage a name.

    The homepage’s title—the line that becomes the browser tab and the blue headline in a search result—is the literal text index.html. The meta description on that page and on all eleven others reads “This website has been created with technology from Avanquest Software,” and the keywords are “WebEasy, Avanquest.” Those are the software vendor’s defaults, never replaced. The interior pages are named the way files on a hard drive are named: SERVICES-1, Vet_owner, Vet_Tech, ccah_Janet, our facility_1. There is no sitemap.xml and no robots.txt—both return 404.

    Opportunity: one honest title and one plain-language description per page, written around what somebody would type into a phone—“exotic vet near Canton MI” should find the page about exotic pets.

  4. 04

    High impact · The part people came for

    Finish the introductions.

    The About page offers five links: veterinarians, vet techs, receptionists, Janet the office manager, and support staff. The links work. Three of the five destinations are a heading and nothing else—the vet techs page is 1,146 bytes and says only “OUR VETERINARY TECHNICIANS”; receptionists and support staff are the same. On the doctors’ pages, the first entry is still “As of January 2019 Dr Tinker has retired. We hope he enjoys a well deserved rest.” For a practice where reviewers name individual doctors, the staff are the product, and most of that section is an empty room.

    Opportunity: a short paragraph and a photograph for each person who works there, on one page rather than five—and the retired doctor moved to a line in the practice’s history instead of the top of the staff list.

  5. 05

    Medium impact · Accessibility

    Let the photographs count.

    There are eighteen photographs on the site—the building, the surgical suite, the prep area, the tour. Not one of them is an <img> element. Every single one is a CSS background applied to an empty <span>, which means none of them can carry alternative text, none can be read aloud, and none can appear in an image search. The homepage also contains no heading elements at all—not one <h1> through <h6>—so a screen-reader user cannot skim it, and the pages still declare the 1990s iso-8859-1 character set.

    Opportunity: real images with real descriptions, a heading structure that matches how the page reads aloud, and modern text encoding—so the same content serves people using a screen reader and Google’s image results at the same time.

  6. 06

    Medium impact · Small broken things

    Fix the emergency link first.

    The VES West referral on the homepage points at http::/www.veswest.com—two colons and one slash. It has never worked. That is the link somebody follows at eleven at night. Alongside it: the MAP link still uses a Google Maps address built in 2013; favicon.ico returns 404 on every page load; the mission statement reads “REASONABLE” on the homepage and “RESONABLE” on the About page; the homepage has said “WE HAVE BEEN IN BUSINESS AT THIS LOCATION FOR 40 YEARS” long enough that a visitor cannot tell how old the practice actually is; and four empty duplicate blocks sit stacked at the same coordinates behind the About panel, left over from an edit.

    Opportunity: repair the referral link today—it is a one-character fix—then work down the rest. None of these individually costs a visitor much; together they are what makes a site feel unattended.

Current experience

The same page, on a desk and in a hand.

On a monitor the layout holds together. The phone capture beside it is the same homepage, on a 390-pixel screen, exactly as a browser renders it—shrunk to 39% because nothing told it not to.

Audit details

What the measurements say.

Taken against the public site on August 24, 2026. A full Lighthouse run could not complete—the host answered the automated request with an HTTP 403—so these numbers were measured directly in Chrome using Lighthouse’s mobile profile: a simulated phone on a 1.6 Mbps connection with a fourfold processor slowdown. Results vary by network, device, cache state, and future content changes.

2.5 s

First contentful paint

Text is on the screen in two and a half seconds on a deliberately slowed phone. This is a real strength and a preservation goal—whatever replaces this site should be held to it, because it is much easier to lose than to earn.

40 KB

Page weight

Fifteen requests, forty kilobytes, no third-party scripts beyond analytics. The entire site across all twelve pages is about 24 KB of HTML. There is nothing here to optimise away—the constraint is what the pages say, not how heavy they are.

39%

Displayed scale on a phone

With no viewport tag, the browser lays the page out at 980 pixels and scales it to fit, giving a visual scale of 0.39. The document is 999 pixels wide, so it also overflows sideways. This single number explains most of the findings above.

0/18

Images with descriptions

Eighteen photographs, none of them an image element, so none can carry alternative text. A complete accessibility assessment would still need keyboard, zoom, contrast and screen-reader testing by hand—this is only what a browser can be asked directly.

Early design exploration

One direction, to make the idea concrete.

This is an exploratory concept, not a final design. It uses the practice’s own hours, address, phone numbers, services, doctors’ own descriptions of themselves, and the photograph of the building already on the site. Nothing clinical was added: the aim is to show ordering and how it behaves on a phone. Final copy, photography, and any wording about medical services would be reviewed with you before anything went live.

Direction 01

Answer the three questions first

Opens with whether you are open, the number to call, and the fact that you see the animals other practices turn away—then introduces the doctors by name.

A sensible path forward

An afternoon, then a week, then a habit.

If Canton Center ever takes this on—with anyone—this is roughly the order I would suggest. The first phase is genuinely an afternoon and would fix the two things that can actually send somebody to the wrong place at the wrong time.

  1. 01
    Stop the misinformation

    Repair the VES West emergency link, settle which set of hours is correct, and update or retire the COVID page. Fix “RESONABLE” while you are in there.

  2. 02
    Make it readable on a phone

    Add the viewport declaration and reflow the layout so the hours, phone number and services are legible at arm’s length without pinching.

  3. 03
    Name the pages and the people

    A real title and description per page, a sitemap, and the staff section finished—photographs and a sentence each, including the technicians.

  4. 04
    Keep caring for it

    Somebody watching hours, holiday closures, staff changes and the referral list, so the site never again disagrees with the front door.
